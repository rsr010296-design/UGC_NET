History of Indian Banking
Key milestones and nationalization
⌄
RBI nationalized in 1949
Imperial Bank nationalized in 1955 and renamed State Bank of India (SBI)
14 commercial banks nationalized in 1969 — this was the first phase of bank nationalization
IDBI (Industrial Development Bank of India) established in 1964

The presidency banks (Bank of Calcutta, Bank of Bombay, Bank of Madras) had acted as quasi-central banks for many years
General Bank of India was an unsuccessful bank dissolved in 1791

Unit 7.2 — Types of Banks
🏦
Bank Categories and Structures
Cooperative, RRBs, Offshore, Foreign, SBI
⌄
Cooperative Banks
Three-tier structure: Primary Agricultural Credit Society → District Cooperative Bank → State Cooperative Bank (apex)
Apex institution under cooperative bank structure is the State Cooperative Bank — commonly tested
Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)
Capital structure: 50% Central Government + 15% State Government + 35% Sponsoring Bank
Governed by the Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976
Chalapathi Rao Committee (2003) was set up for restructuring of RRBs
NABARD is the regulatory authority for all RRBs in India
State Bank of India
SBI came into existence in 1955 when the government took over the Imperial Bank of India and renamed it
Imperial Bank of India was founded in 1806 as Bank of Calcutta
Offshore and Foreign Banks
Offshore Banks — located in low-tax jurisdictions, serve non-residents, offer higher privacy and lower tax burdens
Community Bank — locally operated, empowers employees to make local decisions for customers
Ethical Bank — prioritizes transparency of all its operations

Relationship, Merchant, Indigenous, Development, Nostro/Vostro

Types of Banking
Relationship Banking — Creating, maintaining, and enhancing strong relationships with customers
Merchant Banking — Widening the entrepreneurial base and assisting in a rapid rate of industrial growth

Bank Account Terminology

Income Types

Unit 7.3 — Reserve Bank of India
🛡️
RBI Functions and Key Facts
Roles, subsidiaries, and history
⌄
Functions under RBI Act 1934
Three functions: Banking, Supervisory, and Promotional — Advisory is NOT a function of the RBI
Bank of Issue — has the sole right to issue currency notes
Banker's Bank — provides banking services to all commercial banks
Lender of Last Resort — provides emergency credit to banks
Custodian of foreign exchange reserves and controller of credit
Manages public debt and issues treasury bills on behalf of Central and State Governments
Executes FEMA (Foreign Exchange Management Act) provisions — regulatory and administrative authority
Regulating fiscal policy is NOT a function of RBI — that is the government's role
RBI commenced operations on April 1, 1935
Banking Ombudsman Scheme was introduced in 1995 for customer grievance resolution
RBI worked as Central Bank of Burma till April 1947
RBI worked as Central Bank of Pakistan till June 1948
RBI Subsidiaries (fully owned)
NHB (1987) — National Housing Bank; promotes housing finance institutions (transferred to government in 2019)
DICGC — Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation of India
BRBNMPL — Bharatiya Reserve Bank Note Mudran Private Limited; responsible for printing Indian currency notes
IFTAS — Indian Financial Technology and Allied Services; provides technology services to the banking sector
IIBM, NIBM, and IIBF are NOT subsidiaries of RBI

Monetary Policy Tools
CRR, SLR, Repo, OMO — quantitative and qualitative
⌄
Quantitative (General) Methods
CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio) — Percentage of a bank's total deposits that must be kept with RBI in the form of cash. When CRR is reduced, lendable resources INCREASE.
SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio) — Percentage of net demand and time liabilities that banks must maintain in liquid assets like cash, gold, and government-approved securities
Repo Rate — The rate at which RBI lends money to commercial banks. Higher repo rate = costlier borrowing = less credit creation.
Reverse Repo Rate — The rate at which RBI borrows money from commercial banks. Used to absorb excess liquidity from the system.
OMO (Open Market Operations) — RBI buys and sells government securities in the open market to regulate money supply
Bank Rate — Long-term interest rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks; affects the overall interest rate structure
MSS (Market Stabilization Scheme) — Used to absorb surplus liquidity of enduring nature arising from capital flows; funds held in MSS account with RBI are NOT for normal government expenditure
Qualitative (Selective) Methods
Credit Rationing — RBI sets limits on the amount of credit banks can provide; less commonly used in modern economies
Margin Requirements — Minimum margins on loans against securities; qualitative method
Important: CRR, SLR, Bank Rate, OMO = quantitative; Credit Rationing, Margin Requirements = qualitative
Key Rates Explained Simply
A rise in SLR directly reduces credit creating capacity of banks (banks must hold more liquid assets)
Changes in SLR, unlike CRR, do NOT produce a direct and immediate effect on money supply

Unit 7.4 — Basel Norms, Risk Management and NPA
🔢
Basel Framework — Three Pillars
BCBS, CAR, RWA, capital requirements
⌄
BCBS (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision) was established in 1974 by the central bank governors of ten countries (G10); it now has 45 member countries
Primary objective of BCBS is to standardize and supervise banking regulations to enhance financial stability globally
Pillar I — Minimum regulatory capital requirements based on Risk Weighted Assets (RWAs)
Pillar II — Supervisory review process of capital adequacy
Pillar III — Market discipline through transparency and disclosure requirements
RWA (Risk Weighted Assets) — A measure to determine the minimum capital that must be held by banks; assets are weighted by their risk level
Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) for Indian Public Sector Banks = 12% (as set by RBI under Basel III)
Basel III came into effect in India starting 2013; general minimum CAR is 9%
CCB (Countercyclical Capital Buffer) — restricts participation in system-wide credit booms; can be reduced during economic stress
Tier I capital includes: paid-up capital of equity shares, statutory reserves, perpetual non-cumulative preference shares (PNCPS), innovative perpetual debt instruments (IPDI), and capital reserves from sale of assets
Tier II capital includes: innovative perpetual debt instruments counted above limits, upper Tier-II instruments, lower Tier-II instruments

Non-Performing Assets (NPA)
Classification, management, and related concepts
⌄
An NPA is a loan or advance for which the borrower has NOT made the required payments (principal or interest) for a period of more than 90 days
Sub-standard assets — have been NPA for less than 12 months; higher risk of default
Doubtful assets — have been NPA for more than 12 months; even higher risk
Loss assets — identified as uncollectible by internal/external auditors; of little value to the bank
Regulatory forbearance — banks delay or avoid classifying loans as NPAs; is an emergency medicine, not a staple diet (as stated in the Economic Survey)
Banks classify loans as NPAs based on regulatory criteria (non-payment of interest/principal), NOT solely by auditors' discretion
Capital adequacy norms help strengthen banks' capital base but can restrict sanctioning of more loans — a common Statement II trap in exams
SARFAESI Act 2002 — enables Asset Reconstruction Companies to manage NPA recovery

Unit 7.5 — Financial Markets
📊
Money Market Instruments
Call money, CP, T-Bills, CDs and development
⌄
Call Money — Money borrowed or lent for a single day or overnight between banks; the lending bank earns interest at the call money rate. This is a money market instrument.
Commercial Paper (CP) — An unsecured, short-term promissory note issued by creditworthy corporates, primary dealers, and all-India financial institutions at a discount; maturity 15 days to 1 year in India
Treasury Bills (T-Bills) — Short-term government borrowing instruments issued at a discount; ordinary T-Bills are marketable and can be traded
Ad-hoc Treasury Bills — Issued only in favour of the Reserve Bank of India to create a temporary means of financing; these are NOT marketable
Interbank Call Market — short-term maturity; Commercial Bills — money market instrument; Commercial Paper — promissory note; Treasury Bills — government papers
Development of money market in India sequence: IRSS (1999) → LAF (2000) → Commercial Paper (1990) → WMA linked to bank rate (1997) → RTGS (2004)
Spread in banking is defined as a function of Net Interest Income (NII) — the difference between interest generated by lending and interest paid to depositors
Industrial Securities Market is NOT a money market instrument — it deals with long-term securities

Indices, options, exchanges, and investor protection

Stock Exchanges in India and World
Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) — oldest stock exchange in India; established 1875
National Stock Exchange (NSE) — established 1992
MCX Stock Exchange (MCX-SX) — established 2008

Indices
NIFTY and SENSEX are calculated on free-float market capitalisation — considers only shares available for trading, excluding promoter/government holdings
SENSEX consists of 30 well-established companies listed on BSE
SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India)
SEBI was established in 1992 under the SEBI Act 1992 (NOT the Securities Contract and Regulation Act 1956)
SEBI protects investors' interests and promotes and regulates the securities market
SEBI is NOT constituted from directors of stock exchanges
N.K. Mitra Committee — formed to address investor protection issues in India; submitted report April 2001
Derivatives and Options
American-style options — can be exercised at any time BEFORE the expiry date (more flexibility for holders)
European-style options — can be exercised ONLY on the expiry date
Put option — gives holder the right to SELL a fixed amount of stock at a stated price on or before a predetermined date
Call option — gives holder the right to BUY (purchase) a fixed amount of stock at a stated price on or before a predetermined date
Insider trading is ILLEGAL in the capital market as it involves trading based on non-public material information

Mutual Funds and NBFCs
Structure, roles, regulation, and types
⌄
Mutual Fund Structure
A mutual fund is set up in the form of a trust with four parties: Sponsor → Trustee → AMC → Custodian
Sponsor — is like the promoter of a company; sets up the fund, provides initial capital, and ensures regulatory compliance during setup
Trustee — holds property for benefit of unit-holders; acts as watchdog to ensure mutual fund operates in accordance with SEBI regulations
AMC (Asset Management Company) — manages the funds by investing in various types of securities in a diversified portfolio
Custodian — holds securities in electronic form and facilitates transfer and settlement; is a SEPARATE entity from the mutual fund trust
Mutual funds in India are regulated by SEBI (not AMFI; AMFI is a self-regulatory body)
Entry Load — charge collected from investors when a mutual fund scheme SELLS its units to investors (i.e., when investor buys)
Exit Load — charge collected when an investor SELLS or redeems their mutual fund units
NBFCs (Non-Banking Financial Companies)
NBFCs can be classified as: AFC (Asset Finance Company), IC (Investment Company), LC (Loan Company) — Foreign Trade Company (FTC) is NOT a classification of NBFCs
Shah Committee (1992) — recommended the NBFC regulatory framework
NBFCs are regulated by the RBI Act 1934 and the Banking Regulation Act 1949
Commercial banks serve as HELP TO CONSUMERS; NBFC services include HIRE PURCHASE; Non-depository institutions offer MUTUAL FUNDS; Depository institutions offer MUTUAL SAVING BANK services

Digital Banking and Payment Systems
UPI, RTGS, NEFT, SWIFT, RuPay, e-banking concepts
⌄
E-banking is also referred to as Web-based Banking, Branchless Banking, Virtual Banking, and Internet Banking — all are synonyms
E-banking is essentially regulated by the Information Technology Act, 2000; under this Act, digital signature replaces personal signature
SWIFT — Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunications; used globally for international money transfers
RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement) — transfers money on a real-time and gross basis; minimum transaction limit is ₹2,00,000; no upper limit
NEFT (National Electronic Funds Transfer) — used for transferring funds electronically between bank accounts within India
DD (Demand Draft) — a physical payment instrument; NOT used for international money transfers
RuPay — digital payment system developed and managed by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI)
Skimming — a fraudulent activity where criminals use a small device to steal credit or debit card information during transactions; devices can be placed on ATMs or point-of-sale terminals
Star Topology in banking networks — each node (device) is connected to a central node (hub or switch) by a separate communication medium
Withdrawal of cash from anywhere in India is NOT a feature of Internet Banking — cash withdrawals are done through ATMs or bank branches, not directly through internet banking
IMPS (Immediate Payment Service) objectives: enable mobile banking, build full-range mobile services, simplify payments using beneficiary's mobile number, sub-serve RBI's digital payment goals
UPI (Unified Payments Interface) allows money used for trading to be transferred directly to clearing corporations; it is a real-time payment system

Insurance — Types, Principles and IRDA
History, principles, regulation, and types of insurance
⌄
History of Insurance in India
Insurance in India was first started by Europeans — the Oriental Life Insurance Company was established in Calcutta in 1818 by Europeans
Initially, insurance companies discriminated between Indian and European clients — European clients were charged lower premiums
Bombay Mutual Life Assurance Society (1870) — first Indian life insurance company, covered Indian lives at normal rates
The Indian Mercantile General Assurance was later renamed as Bombay Life (Swadeshi Life)
Prior to 1912, India had no legislation to regulate the insurance business
Malhotra Committee (1993) — recommended a comprehensive framework of reform in the insurance sector including opening to private players
IRDA Act 1999 is the predecessor to Insurance Act 1938 — IRDA was established to regulate and promote the insurance industry and protect policyholders' interests
Principles of Insurance
Principle of Subrogation — after settling a claim, the insurer stands in the insured's place and has the right to recover from an alternative source responsible for the loss
Principle of Insurable Interest — the insured must have a financial interest in the subject matter; they must stand to lose financially if the insured event occurs
Principle of Causa Proxima — the nearest or proximate cause of loss determines the claim; not a remote cause
Principle of Contribution — when a person has multiple insurance policies covering the same risk, all insurers share the compensation proportionately
IRDA Requirements
Minimum paid-up equity capital for life insurance or general insurance business: ₹100 crore
Minimum paid-up capital for reinsurance business: ₹200 crore (NOT ₹300 crore)
Insurance companies must invest NOT LESS than 15% of their funds in infrastructure and social sectors

⌄
